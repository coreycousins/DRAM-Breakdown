01Why Everyone Suddenly Cares About Memory Chips

AI has turned memory chips — usually a sleepy, cyclical corner of tech — into one of the tightest bottlenecks in the entire AI buildout. High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM), the specialized memory stacked onto AI GPUs, is sold out industry-wide through the end of 2026, with its total addressable market projected to reach roughly $100 billion by 2028.

The scramble for HBM capacity is squeezing supply everywhere else too: costs for ordinary server DRAM roughly doubled in the first quarter of 2026 alone, with a roughly fourfold increase expected for the full year. Industry analysts call this the "memory wall" — the point where AI compute (GPUs) has scaled faster than the memory bandwidth needed to feed it, making memory, not raw processing power, the binding constraint on how fast AI systems can be built.

02The Memory Family Tree

Not all memory chips do the same job. A quick map:

  • DRAM (Dynamic Random-Access Memory) — the fast, short-term working memory in every computer, phone, and server. Holds data the processor is actively using; loses everything when powered off.
  • NAND flash — permanent storage. The chip inside SSDs, USB drives, and phone storage; keeps data with the power off.
  • HBM (High-Bandwidth Memory) — a specialized, stacked variant of DRAM, physically bonded on top of AI accelerator chips to feed them data fast enough. Commands roughly a 5-6x price premium over equivalent-capacity ordinary DRAM because of the complex 3D-stacking manufacturing process. This is the product at the center of the current supercycle.
  • NOR flash — an older, slower flash type still used where rock-solid reliability matters more than speed: automotive, industrial, and embedded electronics.
03Who Actually Makes This Stuff

DRAM is one of the purest oligopolies in tech: as of Q2 2026, Samsung holds roughly 39% of the market, SK hynix about 26%, and Micron about 25% — three companies control almost the entire supply. HBM is even more concentrated: SK hynix leads with about 58% share, with Samsung and Micron splitting most of the remainder at roughly 21% each (Q1 2026).

NAND flash is far more fragmented: Samsung (~28%), SK hynix (~19%), Kioxia (~14%), Micron (~13%), and Western Digital/SanDisk (~13%) all compete, alongside China's YMTC (not held in this fund).

Worth watching: CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies), a state-backed Chinese DRAM maker, has climbed the DRAM rankings unusually fast in 2026 — a sign that China is trying to build domestic memory self-sufficiency, which is both a competitive and geopolitical story.

04The Memory Cycle: Historically Brutal Booms and Busts

Memory chips are close to a commodity: a Samsung DRAM chip and an SK hynix DRAM chip of the same spec are largely interchangeable, so price is set by industry-wide supply and demand rather than brand loyalty.

That creates a classic boom-bust pattern: building new fab capacity takes 1-2 years, so producers tend to over-invest when prices are high (adding supply that arrives just as the boom fades) and under-invest when prices are low (leaving the market short when demand next picks up). This lag is why memory stocks have historically been some of the most volatile in all of technology, prone to swinging between shortage-driven price spikes and gluts that crush margins.

05Why This Cycle Might Be Different (Or Might Not)

The AI-driven HBM boom is genuinely structural in a way past cycles weren't: HBM's manufacturing complexity and pricing premium mean makers are dedicating capacity to it that would otherwise have gone to ordinary commodity DRAM, tightening supply across the whole market simultaneously — which is why even non-AI DRAM prices have surged alongside HBM. Some industry analysts (Deloitte among them) think this crunch may not meaningfully ease until 2029.

That said, treat this with healthy skepticism: every previous memory supercycle has eventually ended the same way, with capacity catching up to demand and prices collapsing. The AI story may delay or reshape that pattern — it has not repealed it.
06The Bottleneck Behind the Bottleneck

Even when a company has enough raw memory chips, HBM has to be physically stacked and bonded onto GPUs using advanced packaging techniques (commonly referred to as CoWoS-class processes), a process dominated by Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. (TSMC). And no advanced memory or logic chip gets made at all without EUV lithography machines, which only ASML makes.

These upstream chokepoints mean equipment and foundry companies are often better leading indicators of the memory cycle than the memory makers themselves — an equipment order placed today typically doesn't turn into shipped capacity for 1-2 years, which is exactly why this dashboard tracks TSM, ASML, Applied Materials (AMAT), Lam Research (LRCX), and KLA Corp (KLA) alongside the memory makers.

07How This Fund Is Actually Built

Unlike a simple "buy the memory stocks" basket, DRAM gets most of its exposure through total-return swaps rather than owning shares outright, collateralized by US Treasury bills — which is why its disclosed holdings add up to roughly 125% of net asset value rather than 100%. 08Risks and Things Worth Watching
  • Cyclicality hasn't been abolished. This is still fundamentally a boom-bust commodity market. A capacity overbuild, or any pause in AI infrastructure spending, could turn today's shortage into tomorrow's glut.
  • Customer concentration. A handful of hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta) drive most of the incremental demand for AI memory. A guidance cut from any one of them on data-center capex can move memory stocks sharply.
  • Geopolitics. Nearly every major producer sits in Korea, Taiwan, Japan, or China. Export controls, tariffs, and China's state-backed push for memory self-sufficiency (CXMT) are live risks baked directly into this fund's country exposure.
  • Concentration within the fund itself. The top three issuers (Samsung, Micron, SK hynix) make up roughly three-quarters of DRAM's disclosed economic exposure.

10Glossary
TermMeaning
DRAMDynamic Random-Access Memory — the fast, short-term memory chip in every computer, phone, and server that temporarily holds data the processor is actively using. Loses its contents when powered off.
NANDA type of flash memory that stores data permanently (unlike DRAM) — the chip inside SSDs, USB drives, and phone storage.
HBMHigh-Bandwidth Memory — a stacked, high-speed variant of DRAM built specifically for AI accelerators (like Nvidia GPUs), where moving huge amounts of data quickly is the bottleneck. Currently the highest-margin, fastest-growing corner of the memory market.
NOR flashAn older, slower flash memory type still used where reliability matters more than speed — automotive, industrial, and embedded electronics.
FablessA chip company (e.g. GigaDevice, Phison) that designs chips but outsources actual manufacturing to a foundry, rather than owning its own fabrication plants.
ADRAmerican Depositary Receipt — a US-traded certificate representing shares of a foreign company (e.g. SK hynix's SKHY), letting US investors trade it without a foreign brokerage account.
NAVNet Asset Value — the fund's total assets minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding; effectively the fund's "fair value" per share, which its market price tracks closely for an ETF.
Expense ratioThe fund's annual management fee, expressed as a % of assets (DRAM's is 0.65%/year), deducted automatically from NAV rather than billed separately.
Total-return swapA derivative contract where the fund receives the full return (price moves + dividends) of a stock from a counterparty (here, Goldman Sachs — the "GS" in the swap tickers), without directly owning the shares. Lets the fund get equity-like exposure to hard-to-access or restricted stocks.
Notional exposureThe economic size of a swap position — i.e. how much stock-price movement the fund is exposed to — as opposed to how much cash it actually put up for it.
CollateralAssets (here, US Treasury bills) posted to back a swap obligation, in case the fund owes money to its swap counterparty. This is why DRAM holds so many T-bills despite being a memory-stock fund.
Modified market-cap weightingA weighting scheme based on company size (bigger companies get bigger weights) but with adjustments — here, a 25% cap on any single issuer — to avoid excessive concentration.
